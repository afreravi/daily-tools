What is weighted average cost of capital (WACC)?

The weighted average cost of capital, or WACC, is the average rate a company must pay to finance its assets when every source of capital is blended together. Businesses raise money in two broad ways: equity, by selling ownership shares; and debt, by borrowing. Lenders expect interest payments, shareholders expect dividends and capital appreciation,and neither group gives up its money for free. WACC turns those separate expectations into a single annual percentage rate that reflects the whole company's financing picture.

It matters because it is the yardstick against which investment returns are measured. If a factory expansion promises a 14% return while the company's WACC is 10%, the project creates value; if the same project returned only8%, it would destroy value. Every business decisionthat spends capital - new equipment, acquisitions, marketing campaigns, even holding extra inventory - implicitly gets judged against this blended cost.

How the WACC formula works

The standard WACC formula weights each source of capital by its share of the total capital structure:

WACC = (E / V x Re) + (D / V x Rd x (1 - T))

Here E is the market value of equity, D the market value of debt, V their sum (E + D), Re the cost of equity, Rd the pre-tax cost of debt,and T the marginal corporate tax rate. The equity half is straightforward: multiply the equity weight by the return shareholders require. The debt half gets an extra twist: interest payments reduce taxable income, so the government effectively subsidizes a portion of borrowing costs. That is why Rd is multiplied by (1 - T,:the after-tax cost of debt is lower than the stated coupon. This asymmetry is a big reason many companies lean on debt financing up to a prudent point.

This calculator handles the mechanics for you: it computes the weights, applies the tax shield, sums the two components,and draws a chart showing exactly where the final number comes from.

WACC worked example

Imagine a manufacturer with $600 million of equity market valueand $400 million of debt. Shareholders require a 12% return, lenders charge 6% interest,and the marginal tax rate is 25%. Total capital V is $1 billion, so equity's weight E/V is 0.60and debt's weight D/V is 0.40.

The equity component is 0.60 x 12% = 7.2%. The after-tax cost of debt is 6% x (1 - 0.25 ) = 4.5%,and multiplying by the 0.40 weight gives a debt component of 1.8%. Add the two: 7.2% + 1.8% = 9.0%. This firm's WACC is therefore 9.0%.

Roughly 80% of that 9% comes from equity costsand only 20% from the debt lingering after its tax shield, even though debt is 40% of the balance sheet. The shield makes cheap debt even cheaper, which is exactly whythe debt tax shield shows up so prominently in capital structure analysis.

WACC vs discount rate: what is the difference?

A discount rate is any rate used to convert future cash flows into present value; WACC is one particularly important flavor of discount rate. The words are often used interchangeablyin casual discussion, but they are not synonyms. WACC describes the blended cost of capital for the whole firm's operationsand is the right rate when discounting free cash flow to the firm (FCFF, also called unlevered free cash flow. It sits mid-risk: it averages the cheaper, senior claims of lenders with the pricier, junior claims of shareholders.

Other discount rates fit other situations: discounting free cash flow to equity calls for the cost of equity alone, because debt cash flows have already been subtracted; aprojects with risk very different from the company's average deserves a project-specific hurdle rate capitalized on its own beta;andan private-equity buyer valuing a levered buyout might use a target capital structure rather than the target company's current one. So while your WACC often does double duty as your discount rate, always sanity-check thatthe cash flows being discounted really belong to the whole firm at its average operating risk.

When you actually use WACC

  • DCF valuation. Discount projected free cash flows of the firm at WACC to arrive at enterprise value, then subtract net debt to get equity value. Small errors in WACC compound into large valuation errors, so analysts stress-test their cost of equity assumptions.
  • Capital budgeting. NPV and IRR decisions compare project returns against WACC as the hurdle rate; accept projects that earn more than the blended cost of the money funding them.
  • Mergers and acquisitions. Acquirers model a target's cash flows at an appropriate WACC to price the acquisitionand post-deal WACC shifts asthe combined firm's capital structureand risk profile change. The WACC used should match the target's industry riskand its planned capital structure, not the acquirer's existing one.
  • Performance benchmarking. Economic value added (EVA) is operating profit minus a capital charge computed as invested capital times WACC; positive EVA signals real value creation above the cost of capital.
  • Setting pricing floors. Regulated utilitiesand capital-intensive firms use WACC to justifythe returns embedded in customer prices.

Pro tips for a defensible WACC estimate

  • Use market values, not book values. The formula weights reflect what investors actually paid to ownthe claims today; book equity rarely tracks market equity,and mixing market costs with book weights produces misleading results.
  • Use the marginal tax rate, notthe historical effective rate. The shield applies to the next dollar of interest, so the firm's current marginal rate fits better than a multi-year average thatbakes in one-time items.
  • Keep Rd pre-taxand apply the shield once. Double-counting the tax benefit is a classic error: either use after-tax Rd directly or state Rd pre-taxand multiply by (1 - T,, never both.
  • Don't forget preferred stock. If a company carries preferred shares, add a third term:(P / V) x Rp, where Rp is the preferred dividend yield. Leaving it out understatesthe true blended cost.
  • Recheck the beta. Cost of equity is usually derived from a levered beta via CAPM. Make sure the beta matches the same capital structureas the weights; if not, unleverand relever the beta first.

WACC frequently asked questions

How to calculate your WACC?

Multiply the market value of equity divided by total capital by the cost of equity, then add the market value of debt divided by total capital times the after-tax cost of debt(pre-tax cost of debt times one minusthe tax rate). For example, 40% debt at 6% pre-taxwith a 25% tax rate contributes 40% x 6% x 75% = 1.8% to the total.

How to calculate WACC rate?

Find the market values of equity E and debt D and total V = E + D. Estimate Re (typically via CAPM: risk-free rate plus beta times market risk premium)and Rd (current yield-to-maturity on the debt). Apply the tax shield to Rd, multiply each cost by its weightand sum the two products. The result is your WACC rate.

What does a WACC of 12% mean?

It means the company must earn at least 12% annually on its invested capital to cover the returns its lenders and shareholders collectively require. Investing in assets that return more than12% creates economic value; returning less erodes it, because the firm could have simply returned the capital to its funders instead.

What is WACC in Mero share?

In M&A, WACC is the discount rate for valuing the target's projected unlevered cash flows in a DCF,and it also shapes the post-acquisition capital structure: ifthe combined firm takes on debt, the higher leverage lowers WACC up to a point(more cheap debt,, bigger tax shield,, but rising financial distress risk,. Buyers also comparethe deal's expected return against their own WACC to judge whetherthe acquisition clearsthe cost of the capital funding it.
