Why your title length decides whether anyone clicks

A YouTube title is not a description. It is a single line of text that has to survive three different pieces of machinery: the ranking systems that decide which videos to show, the layout code that decides how much of your line fits, and the human eye scanning a feed at speed. Character count sits at the centre of all three.

When a title overruns the space available, YouTube does not shrink the text or wrap it neatly. It slices the line and adds an ellipsis. Everything after that point is invisible until the viewer taps through. The result is that a carefully crafted payoff, the number, the punchline or the promise, silently disappears for most of your audience.

This checker exists to make that invisible cut visible. As you type, the meter shows your position relative to the 70-character mobile cut-off and YouTube's hard 100-character ceiling, while the previews render your exact wording the way a phone and a desktop search result would.

How the checker works

The tool counts Unicode characters rather than bytes, so accented letters and emoji each register as one character, the same way YouTube's own counter treats them. It then splits your title into letters, digits, spaces, punctuation and emoji, and draws that split as a stacked bar chart so you can see where your budget actually goes.

Four health checks run on every keystroke. The length check compares your total against the 70 and 100 marks. The keyword check looks for repeated words, since stuffing a term twice rarely helps and eats characters. The readability check flags ALL CAPS blocks, which read as shouting and slow down scanning. The hashtag check warns when you push past three tags, because YouTube only displays three above a title and treats a pile of tags as a spam signal.

Nothing leaves your browser. The title you paste stays in the page, and the copy button returns the trimmed text to your clipboard so you can drop it straight into YouTube Studio.

The three numbers worth memorising

100 characters is the hard limit. Type past it and YouTube Studio simply will not accept the title. Nothing about the algorithm rewards filling the full 100.

70 characters is the practical cut-off for mobile. Phone feeds are narrow, and once a title passes roughly seventy characters, the tail starts getting clipped on the devices most of your viewers use.

Around 60 characters is where a title stays fully readable almost everywhere, including cramped search result cards and the related-video sidebar. If you can make your point at 60, you have bought yourself insurance against every layout you cannot see.

There is no length bonus in YouTube's ranking. Watch time moves a video, and the title's only job is to earn the click that starts the clock.

Who gets the most out of it

Creators writing on the fly in Studio use it as a sanity check before hitting publish. Social media managers check titles in bulk when scheduling a month of uploads, because a title that clips at 72 characters on a podcast episode is a small but repeated brand annoyance. Freelance thumbnail designers use the preview panels to confirm the headline on the thumbnail does not repeat the words already visible in the title.

SEO writers use the breakdown chart differently: they watch the space consumed by brackets, pipes and emoji, then decide whether a bracketed series label is worth the fifteen characters it costs. Often it is not.

Pro tips for titles that survive the cut

  • Front-load the promise. Put the specific claim, number or outcome in the first forty characters. If the tail is lost, the title still works.
  • Spend characters on specificity. "We tested 14 budget mics" tells a viewer more than "Budget mic review 2026" and costs the same.
  • Treat emoji as expensive punctuation. One emoji reads as a signal; three read as filler and each costs you a character you could spend on meaning.
  • Move the series tag to the description. A pipe and an episode label can cost a third of your mobile budget for information nobody searches for.
  • Read it out loud. If you stumble, the viewer will too, and a clumsy read is a click lost regardless of length.

Common mistakes and FAQ

What is the maximum YouTube title length?

It is 100 characters for standard videos. Shorts titles are also capped at 100 characters, though Shorts are indexed differently and a very short, punchy title generally performs better than a long one.

Does a keyword-heavy long title rank better?

No. Keyword repetition in the title does not outweigh a weak click-through rate. Modern search relies heavily on engagement signals and on matching a title to what the video actually delivers, so a stuffed title that overpromises tends to suppress rather than lift a video.

Should I write in title case or sentence case?

Either works, but sentence case is easier to scan on a small screen and reads less like a press release. Whatever you choose, keep it consistent across your uploads so your channel feels deliberate.

Why does my title look fine on desktop but cut off on mobile?

Desktop search cards are wider than the phone feed column. A title that fits comfortably at 80 characters on a laptop can already be clipped on a phone, which is why the 70-character mark matters more than the 100-character ceiling for day-to-day publishing.

Do hashtags count toward the character limit?

Hashtags typed into the title field do count as characters. Keep them to three or fewer, and remember that only the first three render above your title regardless of how many you add.
