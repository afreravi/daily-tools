What a discounted cash flow model really answers

A discounted cash flow, or DCF, is the valuation method that asks the most honest question in finance: if this business only ever produced the cash you expect it to produce, what would you pay for it today? Every other approach — comparables, precedent transactions, a price-to-earnings multiple — leans on what someone else paid for something similar. A DCF leans on arithmetic and your own forecast. That independence is its strength and its weakness. Nobody can sell you a narrative in place of a number, but the number is only as good as the assumptions behind it.

The core idea is that a dollar received in five years is worth less than a dollar today, because today's dollar can be invested, and because a promise of future cash carries risk. Discounting translates every projected cash flow into today's terms. Add those present values together and you have the value of the operating business, before any debt or cash on the balance sheet is considered.

How this DCF calculator works

Start with your forecast years. A five-year window is the convention in most models, but anything from three to fifteen works; the longer the window, the more the terminal value assumption gets pushed into the distance. The grid below then shows one free cash flow input per year: the cash the business generates for all capital providers, after the investment needed to keep growing. Negative years are fine and common during a heavy investment phase.

The discount rate is the return you require on the capital tied up in the business. For an enterprise valuation this is usually the weighted average cost of capital, which blends the cost of equity with the after-tax cost of debt. A higher rate says you are less certain about the forecast, and it shrinks the answer quickly: cash flows far in the future are the most sensitive to it.

The terminal value captures everything beyond the explicit forecast, and in most models it is where the majority of the value lives. The calculator offers two standard routes. The perpetuity growth, or Gordon growth, method assumes cash flows keep growing at a steady rate forever — typically close to long-run inflation, because no business outgrows the economy indefinitely. The exit multiple method instead assumes the business is sold at the end of the forecast for a multiple of final-year EBITDA. Both are legitimate; they simply disagree about what happens on the last day.

Subtract net debt from enterprise value and you get equity value, the portion that belongs to shareholders. Enter shares outstanding and the calculator converts that into an implied value per share — the figure you would compare against the market price.

Worked example

Imagine a business with a five-year forecast of $10m, $12m, $14m, $16m and $18m of free cash flow. Discount every year at 9% and the present values run from roughly $9.2m down to $11.7m, summing to about $53m. Now add the terminal value: the fifth year's $18m growing at 2.5% forever, divided by the 9% discount rate minus that growth, gives about $283m, which discounts back to roughly $184m. Enterprise value lands near $237m. If the company holds $20m of net debt, equity value is about $217m. Notice that the terminal value is more than three quarters of the total — that is normal, and it is exactly why the sensitivity grid matters so much.

Reading the sensitivity grid

The grid recalculates enterprise value across a range of discount rates and terminal growth rates around your inputs. Look at how much the value moves between the corners. If a one-point change in the discount rate moves the valuation by a third, your model is not precise enough to justify a confident price target — it is a range, not a number. That is why experienced analysts present a valuation band rather than a single figure.

Pro tips for a credible model

  • Keep terminal growth at or below long-run nominal GDP growth; anything above it implies the business eventually swallows the economy.
  • Make the discount rate consistent with the cash flow — use WACC for unlevered free cash flow.
  • Model the cash flow you can defend, not the one that produces the answer you want.
  • Cross-check the implied exit multiple against what similar businesses actually trade for.
  • Change one assumption at a time so you can see which one is doing the work.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most frequent error is double-counting growth: a healthy growth rate inside the forecast and an aggressive one in perpetuity. The second is discounting cash flows to equity at a WACC built for the whole enterprise, which quietly mixes two different things. The third is forgetting that the terminal value is a promise, not a measurement — it deserves a haircut when the business is young or cyclical. Finally, resist the temptation to keep nudging inputs until the output matches the share price you saw this morning. A DCF should test your assumptions against the market, not flatter them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between DCF and NPV?

They use the same arithmetic. NPV applies it to a single project's cash flows and asks whether the net result is above zero. DCF applies it to a whole business and asks what that business is worth. A DCF is, in effect, an NPV calculation run on a company.

Why is the terminal value so large?

Because a healthy business is assumed to keep generating cash long after the forecast ends. For a going concern, most of the value sits in the years beyond the model. That is expected — and it is why the terminal assumptions deserve the most scrutiny.

Can the terminal growth rate exceed the discount rate?

No. If growth is greater than or equal to the discount rate, the perpetuity formula breaks down and returns a meaningless or infinite value. This calculator rejects those inputs and asks you to lower the growth rate or raise the discount rate.

What discount rate should I use?

For an enterprise valuation, a WACC that reflects the company's actual capital structure and risk. If you are unsure, run the model at several rates — 8%, 10% and 12%, say — and read the sensitivity grid rather than committing to one number.
