What Are Significant Figures?

Significant figures — also called significant digits —are the digits in a measured or calculated number that carry real meaning about its precision. They are how scientists, engineers, and students communicate how carefully something was measured without writing a long essay about the equipment they used. When a scale reads 12.40 grams, then final zero is not decoration:it tells you the measurement was precise to the hundredth of a gram. Write the same value as 12.4 and you have quietly claimed less certainty than you actually had.

The idea matters because numbers in science are rarely exact. Every measurement comes from an instrument with a limited resolution,and every calculation inherits that limitation. Significant figures give everyone a compact, agreed-upon way to show how much of a number is trustworthy —and how much is just rounding noise.

Why Significant Figures Matter in Science and Measurement

Precision is not about showing off digits;it is about honesty. Reporting 3.14159265 when your ruler only supports three digits implies accuracy you do not have,and it can mislead a colleague into thinking your data is far more reliable than it is. In a lab report, an answer with the wrong number of sig figs is a red flag — instructors treat it as a sign that the student does not understand measurement error.

The rules also keep calculations trustworthy. Pro Tips for Using Significant Figures Correctly

  • Write ambiguous numbers in scientific notation. The value 5000 is unclear:it could have one to four sig figs. Spell it out as 5 ×10^3 (one,,5.0 ×10^3 (two,,or 5.000 ×10^3 (four —and nobody has to guess.
  • For addition and subtraction, watch decimal places instead. Addition/subtraction rounds to the fewest decimal places,not the fewest sig figs. This calculator reports both numbers so you can apply either rule.
  • Only round at the end. Carry extra digits through every intermediate step of a calculation,and round once atthe final answer. Rounding early lets small errors compound.
  • Treat zeros carefully in the calculator output. A value like 100.0 has four sig figs,while 100 has one. Adding a trailing decimal point is a legitimate way to show precision — many people forget that trick.
  • Match the least precise input. For multiplication or division,endpoint with as many sig figs as the input that has fewest. If one of your numbers is exact(like a conversion factor with defined value,,treat it as having unlimited sig figs.
  • Use the count to double-check units. When a result looks absurdly precise — like a density calculated from a bathroom scale —then sig fig count reminds you to round it down to a believable number.
