What an acre actually is

An acre is a unit of land area that predates the metric system by centuries, and it survives because it maps neatly onto how farmland was historically worked. One acre is 43,560 square feet, which is also 4,840 square yards or roughly 4,047 square meters. The classic mental image is a rectangle one furlong long and one chain wide — 660 feet by 66 feet — but an acre is a measure of area, not shape. A square acre would be about 208.7 feet on each side, and the same acre can be a long strip, an L-shape, or a circle and still be an acre.

That distinction is where most confusion starts. Buyers and sellers talk about "an acre lot", but the shape of that lot determines whether a house, a driveway, and a septic system will fit on it. Understanding the acreage itself is the first step; understanding the footprint is the second.

How this acreage calculator works

The tool gives you two ways in. The first takes a length and width in feet, meters, yards, or miles and multiplies them to get square footage, then divides by 43,560 to reach acres. That works well for rectangular parcels, and it's a reasonable approximation for irregular fields if you use average width. The second mode takes an area you already know — from a deed, a tax record, or a survey — and converts it between acres, square feet, square meters, hectares, square miles, and square yards.

Alongside the numbers, the results panel puts the figure in context. It shows how many American football fields (including end zones, about 1.32 acres each) your parcel would fill, how long one side of an equivalent square would be, and what the land is worth at the per-acre price you entered. The chart compares your parcel against a quarter acre, one acre, and five acres on a shared scale so you can see immediately whether you're dealing with a suburban lot or a working field.

Where people use it

Property shopping. Listing sites often quote lot size in square feet, while rural listings quote acres. Converting both into one unit makes comparisons honest, especially when lot lines are irregular.

Landscaping and agriculture. Fertilizer, seed, and herbicide labels give application rates per acre or per 1,000 square feet. Knowing the exact area tells you how many bags to buy and prevents both shortfalls and leftovers.

Fencing and utilities. Perimeter costs are driven by shape, not area. Two parcels of identical acreage can differ enormously in fence length, which is why the tool reports the side length of an equivalent square — a useful proxy when only the area is known.

Real estate and development. Zoning rules, minimum lot sizes, and density calculations all reference acreage. Developers also use it to check whether a parcel can be subdivided under local rules.

Solar and site planning. Ground-mounted arrays need spacing per panel row, and quoting that in acres keeps the numbers consistent with land leases.

Common conversions worth memorising

  • 1 acre = 43,560 sq ft = 4,840 sq yd = 0.4047 hectares
  • 1 hectare = 10,000 sq m = 2.471 acres
  • 1 square mile = 640 acres, and one section of the US Public Land Survey is exactly that
  • 1 acre = 0.0015625 square miles
  • A quarter acre = 10,890 sq ft, the classic suburban lot

The 640-acre section is a good sanity check: if your figure comes out at 700 acres, you're looking at roughly one section plus a bit, which is a useful reality check before you sign anything.

Pro tips from people who measure land for a living

Don't trust deed dimensions alone. Deeds describe boundaries, not necessarily area, and recorded dimensions sometimes predate modern surveys. If the acreage matters financially, have it surveyed.

Watch the units inside the numbers. A common error is mixing feet and meters — a 100 m × 200 m field is about 4.94 acres, not 20,000 acres. Convert everything to one unit before multiplying, which is exactly what this tool does for you.

Use average width for irregular shapes. Take width measurements at several points, average them, and multiply by length. For heavily irregular parcels, divide the land into rectangles and triangles, compute each, and add them up.

Remember topography. Aerial-acreage and surface-acreage differ on sloped land. If you're seeding or calculating irrigation, the surface area is what matters.

Check access and usable area separately. Ten acres with a wetland running through it may only offer four buildable acres. The acreage figure tells you the total; a site plan tells you the usable part.

Mistakes that trip people up

The first is confusing acres with square feet when reading a listing — 0.25 acres and 25,000 sq ft are very different things. The second is assuming a rectangular shape for pricing perimeter work, when most parcels are not rectangles. The third is rounding too early: converting to acres, rounding to two decimals, then converting to hectares compounds the error. This calculator keeps full precision internally and only rounds for display, which is why the rounding selector applies to the output and not the maths.

Frequently asked questions

How many square feet are in an acre?

An acre contains exactly 43,560 square feet. That's 4,840 square yards, or about 4,047 square meters. A lot described as 0.5 acres is therefore 21,780 square feet.

How do I calculate acreage from feet?

Multiply the length by the width in feet to get square feet, then divide by 43,560. A 200 ft × 300 ft lot is 60,000 sq ft, which is about 1.38 acres.

Is an acre bigger than a hectare?

No. A hectare is 10,000 square meters, or roughly 2.471 acres, so a hectare is about two and a half times larger than an acre. Both are common in land listings depending on the country.

How many acres is a football field?

An American football field including both end zones covers about 1.32 acres. Without the end zones, the playing field alone is roughly 1.1 acres.

Can I use this for irregular parcels?

Yes, as an estimate. Use average width with the dimension mode, or split the parcel into simpler shapes, calculate each, and add the results with the parcel list.
