What is loan-to-value in a mortgage?

Loan-to-value — usually shortened to LTV — is the ratio between the amount you borrow and the value of the property you are buying or refinancing. It is expressed as a percentage: a $360,000 loan on a $450,000 home is an 80% LTV ($360,000 ÷ $450,000 × 100). In plain terms, LTV tells the lender how much of the home you own outright versus how much the bank is still financing.

LTV is computed at the moment the loan is originated, using the purchase price or the appraised value — whichever is lower. That last detail matters more than people expect: if a home appraises below the agreed price, the effective LTV rises, and the buyer may need to bring more cash to close the gap.

How the LTV ratio is calculated

The formula is deliberately simple:

LTV (%) = Loan Amount ÷ Property Value × ️100

And because the loan amount is usually just the price minus the down payment, the same result can be written as:

LTV (%) = (Property Value − Down Payment) ÷ Property Value × ️100

Three worked examples make the pattern obvious:

  • A $300,000 condo with a $60,000 down payment needs a $240,000 loan: LTV = 80%.
  • The same condo with a $30,000 down payment needs a $270,000 loan: LTV = ️90% — a considerably riskier profile for the lender.
  • A $500,000 house purchased with $200,000 down: LTV = ️60%, leaving you with 40% equity from day one.

This tool applies exactly that math, then labels the result with a risk band so you can see at a glance what the number commonly means in today's mortgage market. The calculation itself is trivial; the judgment about what your LTV implies for pricing, insurance,and approvals is where the real value lies.

What the LTV bands usually mean

  • 60% and below — strong equity. You hold a large stake, typically qualify for the best advertised rates,and often skip private mortgage insurance entirely.
  • 61–80% — conventional sweet spot. The classic 20% down target. You avoid PMI on most conventional loans and unlock competitive pricing from a wide pool of lenders.
  • 81–90% — higher risk. Most conventional loans here require private mortgage insurance, and pricing gets less favorable. FHA-backed loans are common in this zone because they allow smaller down payments.
  • 91–95% — high leverage. Reserved for programs like FHA(3.5% down)and conventional 95% offerings. Expect mortgage insurance and stricter debt-to-income requirements.
  • Above 96% — very high. Only a narrow set of niche programs (VA, USDA, or specialized first-time buyer products) operate here, and the risk band is labeled accordingly.

Treat these bands as general orientation, not quotes. Actual outcomes vary with your credit score, debt-to-income ratio, loan program, property type,and lender appetite.

Pro tips for mortgage planning

  • Use the lower of price or appraisal. Lenders do, and so should you. If the appraisal comes in low, mentally re-run your LTV with the appraised value before negotiating.
  • Watch the 80% line closely. It is the most common PMI removal threshold on conventional loans and a key pricing breakpoint. Sometimes an extra month of saving turns a 82% LTV into an 80% one — and eliminates a recurring insurance bill.
  • Check program-specific limits. FHA, VA,and conventional conforming loans each have special LTV rules, loan limits,and insurance structures. A 97% LTV is normal on a VA loan but essentially unavailable on a standard conforming loan.
  • Re-run the numbers when your home's value changes. In strong markets, appreciation can push your current LTV below 80% faster than your original amortization schedule suggests — that is exactly when refinancing conversations become worthwhile.

Loan-to-value calculator FAQ

What is a good LTV for a mortgage?

For most buyers, 80% or lower is the headline target: it keeps you off private mortgage insurance on conventional loans and opens up the most competitive rates. 90% is still workable with FHA financing, and first-time buyers frequently close at 93–96.5% via specialized programs — they just pay higher insurance costs and narrower lender choice. A "good" LTV ultimately depends on your loan program, credit profile,and monthly budget, but 80% remains the benchmark most planners aim for.

How do I calculate LTV on a home I already own?

Take your current outstanding mortgage balance and divide it by the property's current market value — then multiply by 100. For example, a $210,000 remaining balance on a $350,000 home gives an LTV of 60%. Note that refinance LTV uses a fresh appraisal (or automated valuation), not your original purchase price, so a rising market can improve your ratio without any extra payments.

Why do lenders care so much about LTV?

Because LTV is one of the strongest predictors of default and loss severity. A borrower with a 90% LTV has almost no equity cushion: if prices fall 5–10%, they owe more than the home is worth and may choose to walk away. Lenders price that risk through higher rates, required mortgage insurance,and stricter approval rules. Below 80%, the borrower's own cash is at stake, which aligns incentives and historically drives far lower default rates.

What is combined loan-to-value (CLTV)?

CLTV stacks all secured borrowing against the property — your first mortgage plus any home-equity loan, HELOC, or subordinate lien — divided by the property value. Lenders use CLTV when you open a second loan because it captures the true total exposure. If your first mortgage is at 70% LTV and you add a HELOC equal to 15% of the home's value, your CLTV is 85% — too high for many lenders to allow the second lien.

Is LTV the same as a down payment percentage?

Almost, but inverted. LTV = 100% − down payment percentage. A 20% down payment produces an 80% LTV; a 10% deposit produces a 90% LTV. The two phrases are commonly swapped in real estate listings, so it pays to know both: "we need 20% down" and "the bank wants an 80% LTV" are describing the same requirement from two sides.
