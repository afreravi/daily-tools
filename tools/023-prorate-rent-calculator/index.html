What prorated rent means

Prorated rent is the portion of a month's rent that matches the days you actually occupy a rental. If you sign a lease that starts on the 12th, you are not usually asked to pay for the 1st through the 11th. Instead the landlord divides the monthly rent into a daily figure and charges you only for the days you hold the keys. The same idea applies when you move out before the month ends, or when a tenancy turns over mid-month and the landlord wants to split the final month fairly between two sets of tenants. Prorating is the ordinary, expected way to handle a partial month, and most leases either spell out the method or leave the parties to agree on one.

The phrase sounds technical, but the arithmetic is simple: rent for a whole month, shared across the days of that month, multiplied by your days of occupancy. What causes disagreements is not the concept but the choice of daily rate, because there is more than one accepted way to derive it. This prorate rent calculator removes the guesswork by showing you all of the common methods side by side.

How this prorate rent calculator works

Enter the full monthly rent, the date you move in and the date you move out. The tool counts the days you occupy the unit and applies your chosen method to produce the amount due.

Days in the calendar month. The daily rate is the monthly rent divided by the number of days in that particular month — 28, 29, 30 or 31. Your charge is the daily rate times the days occupied. This is the most common method because it is the most literal reading of "a month's rent."

Flat 30-day month. The daily rate is the monthly rent divided by 30, regardless of the calendar. It is simple and predictable, and some leases and letting agents prefer it for exactly that reason. In a 31-day month it slightly overcharges; in February it undercharges.

Days in the year. The daily rate is twelve months of rent divided by 365 (or 366 in a leap year). This method treats every day of the year equally and is common where rent is expressed annually, but it produces a daily rate that does not line up with any single month.

The tool counts the move-out day as occupied by default, matching the way leases are usually read, and offers an option to exclude it if you hand back the keys that morning. It also draws a bar chart so you can see at a glance how far apart the three methods land, and prints the exact formula behind each figure.

When you will need this

  • Move-in mid-month. Your first rent payment covers only the days from your start date to the end of that month.
  • Move-out mid-month. Your final payment covers the days up to and including your last day, not the whole month.
  • Split a turnover month. When one tenant leaves on the 15th and another arrives on the 16th, each pays for their own days.
  • Short-term or extended stays. Guests who stay part of a month can be charged a fair share rather than a full period.
  • Checking a landlord's figure. If a statement looks off, run the numbers yourself before you query it.

Pro tips

  • Agree the method in writing first. A few dollars of difference is not worth a dispute, but confirming the daily-rate method in the lease prevents one.
  • Read the lease wording. "Prorated rent" often names the method, which settles the argument instantly.
  • Watch February. A days-in-month method makes February the most expensive month per day and the flat 30-day method the cheapest; check the effect before signing a short lease.
  • Count only occupied days. Days you cannot legally use the unit, such as a period when repairs make it unlivable, should not be charged.
  • Keep the calculation. Save the breakdown with your receipt so any later query is easy to resolve.

Common mistakes and FAQ

How do you prorate rent?

Divide the monthly rent by the number of days in the month to get a daily rate, then multiply by the number of days occupied. For example, $1,800 in a 30-day month is $60 a day, so 12 days costs $720. The calculator does this automatically and also shows the 30-day and annual variants.

Do you count the move-out day?

Usually yes for the last day of occupancy, because you still hold the unit that day. Some leases exclude it if the keys are returned in the morning. The tool lets you switch between the two so you can match your agreement.

Should rent be divided by 30 or by the days in the month?

Either can be correct, depending on the lease. Dividing by the actual days in the month is the most common and the most literal. Dividing by 30 is simpler and predictable. If the lease is silent, the days-in-month method is the safer default.

Is prorated rent legal?

Yes. Prorating is a normal and lawful way to charge for a partial month, and many jurisdictions expect it when a tenancy starts or ends mid-month. Local landlord-tenant rules vary, so check your own state or country guidance for specifics.

Does prorating apply to the last month too?

It normally does. If you vacate before the month ends and have paid the full month, you may be owed a refund for the unused days, calculated the same way.
