What a trapezoid actually is

A trapezoid is a four-sided shape with exactly one pair of parallel sides. Those parallel sides are called the bases — usually written a and b — and the perpendicular gap between them is the height, h. The other two sides are the legs. This calculator takes your bases and height and returns the area immediately, then goes a step further by reporting the perimeter, the median line, and a drawn-to-scale picture so you can sanity-check the number before you use it.

One quick note on vocabulary, because it trips people up on exams and on job sites. In the United States, "trapezoid" means exactly one pair of parallel sides. In the United Kingdom and much of the Commonwealth, the same shape is a "trapezium," and what Americans call a trapezium is the quadrilateral with no parallel sides at all. The maths is identical; only the label changes.

How the trapezoid area formula works

The formula is Area = (a + b) ÷ 2 × h. It is worth understanding where it comes from rather than memorising it. Take two identical trapezoids and rotate one by 180 degrees. Fit them together along their legs and you get a parallelogram whose base is the sum of the two original bases (a + b) and whose height is still h. The area of that parallelogram is (a + b) × h, and since it is made of two of your trapezoids, each one is exactly half of it. That is the whole formula.

There is a second way to read it. The quantity (a + b) ÷ 2 is the average of the two bases, which is also the length of the mid-line or median — the segment that runs halfway between the parallel sides and joins the midpoints of the legs. So the formula says: area equals the median times the height. Once you see it that way, the answer usually becomes obvious. A trapezoid with bases 10 and 6 behaves, area-wise, exactly like a rectangle 8 wide and 4 tall.

A worked example using the values pre-filled in the tool: bases of 10 cm and 6 cm with a height of 4 cm. The average base is (10 + 6) ÷ 2 = 8 cm. Multiply that by the height and you get 8 × 4 = 32 cm². The same answer falls out of the long form — (10 + 6) × 4 ÷ 2 = 64 ÷ 2 = 32. Change any one of the three inputs and the area moves with it; doubling the height doubles the area, while doubling both bases quadruples it.

Perimeter, legs, and the rest of the shape

Area is only one property. The perimeter is simply a + b + c + d, where c and d are the legs. If you know the legs, type them into the optional fields and the tool adds them up for you. If you do not know them but the trapezoid is isosceles — the common case in school problems and in most symmetrical designs — you can derive them.

For an isosceles trapezoid, drop a perpendicular from each end of the shorter base to the longer base. That carves the shape into a central rectangle and two identical right triangles. The horizontal run of each triangle is (a − b) ÷ 2 and the vertical rise is h, so Pythagoras gives the leg as the square root of h² plus ((a − b) ÷ 2)². The tool applies exactly this when the isosceles box is ticked. Untick it if your legs are genuinely different lengths and you will supply them yourself.

Two more details worth knowing. A trapezoid with one leg perpendicular to the bases is a right trapezoid — its height equals that leg, which makes it the easiest version to measure on site. And any quadrilateral whose diagonals are perpendicular and whose median length equals the height is a special case that satisfies the area formula for a kite, so do not assume every four-sided shape with a "height" is a trapezoid.

Real-world uses

Roofing is the most common one. A hip roof plane is a trapezoid, and roofers quote area to order shingles, underlayment, and flashing. Landscaping and civil work come next: a trapezoidal drainage channel, a road embankment, or a retaining wall cross-section is priced by volume, which starts with the trapezoid area multiplied by length. Estimating a trapezoid's area is also routine when you are calculating gravel or topsoil for a driveway that tapers from a wide street entrance to a narrow gate.

In the classroom, the trapezoid is where students first meet the idea that a formula can be rearranged rather than only plugged into. Solve for the height and you get h = 2A ÷ (a + b); solve for a base and you get b = (2A ÷ h) − a. This tool handles the height case directly when you tick "I know the area instead."

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Using the slanted leg as the height. The height must be perpendicular to the bases. If you measure along the leg, your area comes out too large.
  • Forgetting to halve the base sum. (a + b) × h is the area of the doubled parallelogram, not of the trapezoid.
  • Mixing units. Convert everything to one unit first. Bases in inches with a height in feet will produce a nonsense area.
  • Assuming all four sides are needed. Area needs only the two bases and the height. Extra sides are irrelevant to the area calculation.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for the area of a trapezoid?

Area = (a + b) ÷ 2 × h, where a and b are the parallel sides and h is the perpendicular height between them. Equivalently, median times height.

How do I find the height if I only know the area?

Rearrange to h = 2A ÷ (a + b). Enter the area and both bases in this tool with the "solve for the height" option ticked and it will do the division for you.

Is a trapezoid the same as a trapezium?

In US usage, yes — both words refer to the one-pair-of-parallel-sides shape. In UK usage they are different: a trapezium has no parallel sides. Always check which convention your reference uses.

Can a trapezoid have a right angle?

Yes. If one leg is perpendicular to both bases, the shape is a right trapezoid and it has two right angles. Its perpendicular leg doubles as the height.

What is the median of a trapezoid?

The median is the segment joining the midpoints of the legs. Its length is the average of the bases, (a + b) ÷ 2, which is why area can be written as median × height.

Does the area change if I flip the trapezoid?

No. Rotating or reflecting a shape never changes its area. Only the base lengths and the perpendicular height matter.
